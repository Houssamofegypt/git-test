Reading Below the Dots

Ten Arabic editions across eight riwāyāt, 130,030 morphological segments, and a normalization ladder whose bottom rung is the undotted consonantal skeleton — the layer manuscript research actually operates at.

01 — Why this shape

The text is not the hard part

It is 800 KB. Storing it is not a problem, and anyone who frames the task as “import the text into a database” builds something that works for a week and then quietly starts lying.

The actual problem: every interesting question about the Quran is a question about spans of text, and every scholarly disagreement is a disagreement about which text, which segmentation, or which normalization. Word frequency depends on where words begin. Root frequency depends on a morphological analysis someone had to make. “Is this phrase unique?” depends on whether you compared vocalized text, consonantal text, or a bare skeleton. Get one wrong, once, in a helper function — and every number downstream is wrong in a way that never surfaces as an error.

So the infrastructure has one job: make the text immutable, make addressing stable, and make every derived fact reproducible and attributable. Raw sources are pinned to a commit and a SHA-256. The database is a build artifact that reproduces to an identical content digest. All annotation is stand-off — nothing ever writes into the text. And normalization is a ladder, not a destination.

02 — The ladder

Six rungs, all stored

A query declares which rung it ran on. Nothing is destroyed, because the rung above is always still there.

The tinting below is not decoration. In the manuscript tradition the consonantal skeleton was written in ink and the vowel points were added later, in red, by a different hand. The page colours them the same way — and which character belongs to which class is read from the build, not decided in the browser.

03 — Critical apparatus

Readings, not spellings

Editions are rows, not columns. That is what lets eight riwāyāt sit side by side and their differences become a queryable table.

The split that matters is reading versus orthography. imlaei-simple and indopak transmit the same Ḥafṣ reading in a different spelling convention; filing them next to Warsh under one heading is how orthography gets published as textual variance. They get their own bucket.

04 — Undotted skeletons

What a bare codex underdetermines

In a bare Ḥijāzī codex, بنت, ينبت, ثبت and تنبت share one skeleton.

Any question of the form “could this reading have been misread as that one?” is a question about archigraphemic identity. No off-the-shelf Arabic normalizer computes it, so quranlab does — position-sensitively, because final nūn and final yāʾ are distinct shapes while their medial forms are not. 1,350 word skeletons in the reference edition admit more than one dotted reading.

05 — Validation

A result nobody needs

Pairwise consonantal distance across the riwāyāt recovers the transmission tree. Every riwāyah’s nearest neighbour is its sibling from the same qāriʾ. Four for four.

This has been known for a millennium. It is here as a tripwire: if the rasm rung ever folds the wrong things together, this structure dissolves into noise — and a normalization bug announces itself as a failing claim instead of as a plausible-looking finding three papers later.

06 — Findings

Four things that were silently wrong

None of these raised an error. All four would have produced publishable-looking numbers.

1,741 of 6,236 ayahs

The typographic space

The KFGQPC text separates a tanwīn from its silent alif with a real U+0020 — a rendering device, not a word boundary. Naïve tokenization put 28% of ayahs at the wrong word count, and every one still looked fine on screen. Visible in Figure 1: compare raw to plain at 2:2.

13,370 characters

Invisible contaminants

U+200F right-to-left marks scattered through the editions so they render correctly when pasted into a left-to-right document. Two strings look identical, compare unequal, and nothing on screen explains why.

~6,000 phantom variants

Yeh barree

Warsh and Qālūn spell final yāʾ as U+06D2 — typography, not a consonantal choice. Unfolded it manufactured thousands of fake differences between riwāyāt. Folding it cut Warsh’s consonantal variance from 6,486 to 3,090.

f(f(x)) ≠ f(x)

Non-idempotent normalization

Deleting a combining mark can leave its neighbours out of canonical order, so whether two texts compared equal depended on how many times each had been normalized. Every rung now re-applies NFC after filtering.

07 — The claim ledger

A finding is a sentence plus its query

Claims live in claims/*.toml and are re-run on every build. A claim that stops reproducing fails CI — because the sources moved, the normalization changed, or the claim was wrong.

This is the piece that makes the repository research infrastructure rather than a nicely-shaped copy of the text.

08 — Refusals

Twelve disagreements, kept

Where the reference edition and the morphology corpus genuinely disagree about where words begin, the ayah is recorded rather than smoothed over. A silent 1-in-500 misalignment is how a corpus starts lying.

09 — Running it

This page is the showcase, not the instrument

Everything above is a static export. The working tool queries the database directly and does things this page cannot.

# the whole pipeline: fetch pinned sources, build, verify, re-run every claim
git clone https://github.com/Houssamofegypt/git-test
cd git-test && make

# the local explorer — full database, projected search, every edition
make serve

Two things the static build deliberately cannot do. Search here matches your query literally against a stored rung; the local build projects it onto that rung first, so الله at rasm also finds ٱللَّه. And the 249,402 vocalization-only variants are summarised as counts rather than shipped — the consonantal ones, which distinguish readings, are all here.

The annotation table is empty on a fresh build. That is deliberate: it is where research goes, and baking a scholarly position into the canonical store would make it look like a fact.
